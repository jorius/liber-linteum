# 03 The Salt Road

The quartermaster was a short, sun-cured woman with a slate on a cord round her neck and a voice that never stopped counting. She was counting when Ama found her, standing in the half-dark of the caravan yard with a lamp in one hand, touching each pack-saddle as she passed it: "thirty-one, thirty-two, that one's loose, thirty-three."

"I need to get to Glassreach," Ama said.

"Most people do, eventually. Thirty-four." The woman looked her up and down: the grey Keeper's robe, the satchel, the cord on her cuff. "Loom House pays the Keepers' fare at the Glassreach end. You'll walk when the camels walk and sleep when they sleep, and if you lose anything of mine I'll knot it into your debt myself. Dela Fenn." She held out a hand that was all calluses. "Quartermaster. Thirty-five."

That was how Ama went down the [[The Salt Road|Salt Road]].

---

They left before the sun, forty camels and twenty-two drovers and a wake of dust that hung pink in the air behind them for an hour. The road was not really a road. It was a line of cairns across the dunes, one every few hundred paces, each stone set by some caravan long ago and reset by every caravan since, so that the way was always there even when the wind had buried the ground. On the map in the Loom House it was a neat dotted line from the Courts to Glassreach. On the ground it was heat, and light, and the creak of saddles, and Dela's voice somewhere up ahead, counting.

On the first evening the caravan left the cairns.

"Varn business," Dela said, when Ama asked. "Half a day east. We do it every run."

[[Hollow Stone]] was an outcrop of black rock rising out of the dunes like the knuckles of a buried fist, with a Varn post built against its foot and a Varn flag hanging limp above it. They came to it after dark. Ama had expected a cistern. What she saw, by the light of the guards' lamps, was a pipe of thick green glass coming out of the rock at the height of a man's chest, and water pouring out of it.

Not dripping. *Pouring*, in a rope as thick as her wrist, into a stone trough, and from the trough into the casks the drovers rolled up one by one. The sound of it was the strangest thing she had ever heard. On Kessari water was something you poured from a jar into a cup, carefully, with both hands. She had never in her life heard water simply *running*, as if there were more of it than anybody needed.

The guards sealed each cask with a disc of blue salt pressed with the Varn hexagon. Dela had a beam scale set up at the edge of the lamplight. She weighed every cask as it came off the trough, and every time she did, her fingers moved at the cord she wore beside her slate.

Ama watched the fingers. She knew those knots. Not the plain message knots drovers tied, but the clusters with the thumb-wide gaps. Keepers' knots.

---

She said nothing about it for three days. On the fourth day they came to the [[Cairn of Teeth]].

It was a place where black rock broke out of the sand in a long, jagged line, like the jaw of something enormous lying just under the dunes. The caravan stopped there to water the camels from its own casks, not the sealed ones. Ama walked away from the noise to where the biggest cairn stood, at the end of the rocks, and stood in front of it, and found that she did not know what she had come to do.

There was no grave. There was nothing to bury. *Lost on the Salt Road.* She had been seven. She remembered a great many people telling her in kind voices that sandfalls were quick.

After a while Dela came and stood beside her and didn't count anything.

"I was on that run," Dela said. "Twelve years back. The Dry Year. I was a cask-girl, sixteen, so green I still thought the camels liked me." She looked at the rocks, not at Ama. "There was a Keeper with us. Tall, laughed at her own jokes. She had a roll in a case she wouldn't let anyone else carry. Every night, while the others slept, she'd go and look at the Varn casks."

Ama's throat had closed. She nodded.

"One night I followed her, to see what was so interesting. She caught me, of course. I thought she'd send me back. Instead she sat me down by the scale and said, *If you're going to be nosy, child, be nosy properly.* And she taught me to tie these." Dela lifted the cord beside her slate. "Weigh it. Knot it. Never trust a number you didn't tie yourself. She said that every night for a week."

"And then?"

"And then one night a rider came in from the east, in Varn blue, and talked with the caravan master by the fire a long while. And in the morning the Keeper was gone, and the roll with her, and the wind had been at the sand all night, so there were no tracks either way." Dela was quiet a moment. "Sandfall, they said. The road takes whom it takes."

Ama turned and stared at her.

"That's what the master said," Dela said. "That's what the rider said to him the night before; I heard it through the canvas. It stuck with me. It's a thing people say about the road, but I'd never heard anyone say it before somebody was lost."

---

That night Dela came to Ama's blanket with a leather bag and set it down beside her.

"Six years," she said. "Every Varn cask that's gone through my yards since I took the slate. Weighed and knotted, the way she taught me. I don't break seals. I don't ask what's in them. I'm a quartermaster, not a fool. But I count what's under them." She pushed the bag across. "Varn draws eleven hundred drams a fortnight on the Courts' linen. I put fifteen, sixteen thousand of theirs on the road every fortnight, sealed. Either their camels are making water, or somebody is."

Ama put her hand on the bag. Through the leather she could feel the cords inside, coil on coil of them, six years of somebody's patience.

"Why give them to me?"

"Because she was your mother," said Dela Fenn. "You have her laugh. You haven't used it once since we left the yards, but you have it." She stood, and brushed sand off her knees, and was already counting again as she walked away. "Seemed only right they went back to a Seyru."

Overhead, Thane went racing across the sky on its quick red errand, and the dunes lay silent all the way to the edge of the world.
