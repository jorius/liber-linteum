# 05 The Closing

They came over the last ridge on the afternoon of the Dusk Closing, the camels stumbling, the drovers grey with dust, Dela Fenn hoarse from counting. Below them the Courts lay on their terraces in the heat, and beside them the round stone mouth of the [[Well of Osk]], and beside that the long low roof of the Loom House, its courtyard already strung with lines for the airing.

Mother Hesk was standing in the courtyard among the empty lines, with Ama's knotted message in her hand. She looked as if she had been standing there for twenty-five days.

Ama walked across the courtyard and stopped in front of her. She did not take off her satchel. She laid it out, quietly, the way you lay out a ledger for an audit: the knots on her sleeve, the strip from the stacks, Sefa Imre's copy with the Annex witness knot, Dela's bag of cords. She told her about Hollow Stone, and the glass pipe, and the sound of running water. She told her what Dela had heard through the canvas at the Cairn of Teeth, and what Teodor Varn had said at the foot of the Annex stair.

When she had finished, Hesk was quiet so long that the Keepers on the far side of the courtyard, hanging the first of the ledgers, stopped pretending not to watch.

"It was my hand," Hesk said.

Ama had known. She had known since the lamp went low in the Loom House twenty-seven days ago and her eyes went to the hook of a seven. It did not help at all.

"Orla Varn came to the Loom House eleven days after the Cairn," Hesk said. "She brought the twin. Your mother's roll. I suppose the rider gave it to her. She set it on my table and said the Keeper who had carried it had been very unlucky. She said the Loom House drank Court water like everyone else, and the Courts were frightened, and frightened people cut what they don't understand. And she said that children are lost on the Salt Road every year." Hesk looked down at her own hands. "You were seven. You were asleep in the next room."

"So you rewrote them."

"Six years of Rise rows, in the hands of the Keepers who had written them. It took me most of a year of nights. Orla watched me burn the twin." Hesk's mouth moved strangely. "I rewrote the ink. I left the knots. I told myself that was courage. It was only a smaller cowardice."

"Why leave them?"

"Because to cut them I would have had to cut every cord in six years of rolls, and every Keeper in the house would have seen." She lifted her eyes at last. "And because I hoped someone would read them one day. Someone braver than me. I never hoped it would be you. I hoped very hard that it would not be you."

Ama found that she was crying, which was stupid, because she was not sad. She was something much larger than sad, and it had nowhere to go.

"It isn't enough," Hesk said, more briskly, wiping her own face with the heel of her hand as if it were a smudge of ink. "Knots and twins and a quartermaster's cords. The Courts will listen politely, and then they'll ask Pell Odrin what the well says, and Pell will tell them what the well says at low moon." She looked past Ama, at the round stone mouth beyond the wall, and something in her face set like mortar. "So we'll have to let the well speak for itself. Come."

---

Nobody had gone down the Well of Osk at a Closing since the Dry Year. The Courts had forbidden it after a young wellwright drowned on the stair, on Orla Varn's motion, *for the safety of the guild*. The gate at the head of the stair was locked, and the key hung on [[Pell Odrin]]'s belt.

He listened to Hesk without interrupting. He had known her for fifty years. Then he took the key off his belt and weighed it in his palm.

"I've measured this well at low moon for twelve years because the Courts told me to," he said. "Before that I went down at every Closing, and my master before me, and his master before him, and we scratched the high water on the glass. I haven't seen the high water in twelve years. I'd like to, before I die." He fitted the key in the lock. "It's my well. I'll decide who goes down it."

The stair wound down the inside of the shaft in a long tight spiral, cut into the glass lining itself. The glass was the same as Glassreach's, carried here in blocks from the Flats four hundred years ago and fused in place, and Pell's lamp turned it gold near the top and then green, and then a deep cold blue like the inside of a wave. All the way down, scratched into the glass at the edge of the stair, were the marks of the old wellwrights: a line, a date, two initials, a line, a date, centuries of them, crowded together in a band where the high water had always come.

At the bottom the water lay black and perfectly still, a long way below the oldest marks. Pell chalked its level on the glass. "Low-moon reading," he said. "Same as I gave the Courts."

Then they waited on the lowest landing, the three of them, with the lamp turned down.

They felt the moons rise before they heard anything. It was like the moment before a sneeze, or a storm. Then, from very far above, down the whole length of the shaft, came the sound of the dunes beginning to sing: a low note, too low to be a note, the sound of a whole desert humming to itself with its mouth closed. The glass took it up. It hummed under Ama's palms and in the stair under her feet and in her back teeth, and she understood at last why Glassreach stood still to listen.

The water began to rise.

It did not rush. It came up the shaft the way a held breath comes up through a body, smooth and certain, and they climbed ahead of it, landing by landing, watching it follow them. It passed Pell's chalk mark without pausing. It passed a scratch that said *401: Dry Year*, and went on. It climbed the glass through twelve years of nothing, through the place where it ought to have stopped if the well were dying, and still it rose, past landing after landing, into the band of old lines.

It stopped a finger's width below the highest of them. The highest of them was a line of salt, white and crusted, laid down by four hundred years of Closings, and the water stood just under it and trembled with the dunes' song, as full as it had ever been.

Pell Odrin said a word Ama had never heard a man of his age say. Then he held the lamp out over the water and said it again, more quietly.

Just below the salt line, on the far side of the shaft, there was a hole in the glass.

It was round, a little wider than a man's shoulders, its edges ground smooth with great care. It was not wellwright work. And the water, wherever it touched the lip of it, was sliding into it in a smooth, dark, steady tongue, the way water slides over a weir. Ama thought of a glass pipe in a black rock half a day east, and of the sound of water simply running, as if there were more of it than anybody needed.

She knelt on the landing, paid out the measuring cord from her satchel down the glass until its weight touched the surface, and tied a knot at the rim of the landing: the high water of the Dusk Closing of 413, in Keepers' knots, where no ink could get at it. Beside her Pell took the diamond stylus from his belt and, with a hand that hardly shook, scratched a line into the glass at the water's edge, and beside it: *Dusk Closing 413. P.O. saw it.*

Hesk sat down on the stair. Her face was wet again, and she did not wipe it.

"Twelve years," she said. "It was here every fortnight. And I never once came down to look."

---

They came up into the grey of dawn. Ilu and Thane were setting together over the dunes on the far side of the town, two pale coins sliding into the sand, and low moon was coming, and with it the sitting.

[[Teodor Varn]] was waiting by the gate at the head of the stair with two of his house's guards. He looked at Pell's wet sleeves, and at Ama's cord, and at Hesk.

"You went down at the Closing," he said. "That's forbidden."

"Not to the Wellwright," said Pell Odrin.

Teodor considered him for a moment. Then he stepped aside, and the guards stepped aside with him.

"I'll see you at the sitting, then," he said. And, as Hesk passed him: "Eldest."

"Heir," said Mother Hesk, and did not look at him at all.
