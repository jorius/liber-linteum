# 06 What the Glass Remembers

Word had gone round the town in the night the way water goes round a dry pot, finding every crack. By the time the lamps were lit along the Terraces the hall was full past its arches: stewards and drovers and wellwrights in their leather aprons, Keepers in grey along the back wall, Dela Fenn's whole caravan crowded into the doorway still smelling of camel. The seven houses sat in their horseshoe in their seven colours, and nobody in the hall was talking above a murmur.

On the Keepers' bench Mother Hesk opened the ledger case, looked at the rolls inside it for a moment, and then closed it again and set it on Ama's knees.

"You read," she said.

---

Before any sealing, the Keeper reads the sums. It is the oldest rule of [[The Salt Courts]], older than the Terraces: no debt may be sealed on linen until the houses have heard, aloud, the numbers it rests on. Most sittings it took a few minutes and nobody listened. The Speaker called for the sums on the Glassreach cut, and Ama stood up, and found that her knees were perfectly steady.

She read the ink. Twelve years of the Rise, Closing by Closing, sinking. The hall knew those numbers; it had heard them all its life. She read them to the end, and rolled the ledger closed, and said:

"Those are the numbers in ink. Now I'll read you the knots."

There was a noise from the horseshoe like a sheet of linen tearing. The Speaker raised a hand, and looked at the Keepers' bench, and then at the back wall, where forty grey robes stood without moving.

"The Keeper's reading is the Keeper's," he said at last. "Go on."

So she read them the knots. The Rise of 396 and 397 and 398, of 399 and 400, the strong steady well under the sinking ink. She held up Sefa Imre's copy of the Glassreach twin and showed them the Annex witness knot, and read them that too, and the numbers were the same numbers. She opened Dela's leather bag and read them six years of Varn casks, weighed and knotted at the yards: fifteen thousand drams in a fortnight, sixteen, sixteen and a half.

"Every fortnight for six years," Ama said, "the Rise as the Courts reckon it has come up short of the knots by about sixteen thousand eight hundred drams. Every fortnight for six years, about sixteen thousand drams of House Varn's water has gone down the Salt Road under seal. The difference is what spills, and what House Varn drinks." She found she had nothing to add to that, so she didn't.

[[Teodor Varn]] let the silence run exactly as long as it was useful to him. Then he stepped out from behind his mother's stool.

"I have nothing but respect for the Loom House," he said, and sounded as if he meant it. "So I'll be careful. What we've heard is a grieving young woman, a quartermaster's string, and a copy of a copy that none of us can check without eighteen days on a camel. And knots." He smiled at the back wall, apologetically. "Forgive me. But knots are what Keepers tie when the lamp is low and the night is long. The well has been falling for twelve years. Every one of you has heard the readings. Pell Odrin has given them to you himself."

"I count casks," said Dela Fenn from the doorway.

Heads turned. She held up her slate.

"That's all. I count casks. I've counted Varn's for six years. Anyone who'd like to check my count can come down to the yards and weigh them with me. Bring your own scale."

[[Pell Odrin]] stood up before anyone could answer her.

"The readings are true," he said, in his rope-and-pulley voice. "They're low-moon readings. They're true at low moon." He looked round the horseshoe, house by house. "Last night I went down my well at the Closing, for the first time in twelve years. The water came up to the old salt line, where it came every Closing before the Dry Year. A finger short of it, no more. I always thought the old rolls read low, against what I remembered. But I'm an old man, and memory isn't linen, and nobody asked me. And there's a hole in my well's glass, a little below that line, wide as a man, that I didn't cut and no wellwright cut, and the high water runs into it like beer into a drunk." He sat down. "Any head of house can come down at the next Closing and see both. The line will be there. So will the hole, unless somebody fills it."

Nobody said anything at all. Then old Benedek of House Ossary, who had objected to the cut twenty-seven days ago, though not very hard, leaned forward on his stool.

"Then who," he said, "rewrote the ink?"

Mother Hesk stood up.

She told it as plainly as she had told it in the courtyard, in her small, upright voice, and did not spare herself a word of it: the twin set on her table, the children lost on the Salt Road every year, the year of nights, the hooks pushed the wrong way. She named [[Orla Varn]]. She did not raise her voice to do it. At the end she said, "I ask the Courts to strike my name from the Loom House," and sat down, and folded her hands.

Every eye in the hall went to the blue stool.

Orla Varn had not moved in the whole sitting. Now she lifted her head, and for the first time Ama heard her speak.

"When the water fell in the Dry Year," the old woman said, "I saw the end of everything. Every one of you did. I was simply the one who did something about it. I made sure my house would outlive the well." Her voice was dry and thin and perfectly calm. "Then the water came back. And it seemed such a waste to tell anyone."

---

The vote was not long.

Ossary moved it and Quorra seconded it. The cut to Glassreach was struck from the roll. The hole in the glass was to be sealed by the wellwrights before the next Closing. The seat of House Varn was to stand dark until its debt was paid, in drams, to Glassreach, at a reckoning the Keepers would make and knot and read aloud at every sitting until it was done. And the stair of the Well of Osk was to stand open at every Dusk Closing, to anyone at all who wished to go down and look.

Six houses said aye. Lirr said it late, and quietly. Hadrel, who had not spoken in a sitting for as long as anyone could remember, said it loudly enough to make the lamps jump. The Varn stool said nothing.

Afterwards, while the hall emptied out into the white morning, Teodor stopped at the Keepers' bench, where Ama sat writing the vote onto the sitting roll.

"You think the truth will keep the peace," he said. It was not quite a question.

"No," said Ama. "I think it's owed." She dipped her pen. "Keeping the peace is your trade. Mine is writing down what's owed."

He looked at her a moment longer. Then he went out after his mother, a dark-blue shape walking into the light, and did not look back.

Ama finished the line. The vote had been taken at the low moon after the seventeenth Closing of 413, and she wrote the date in numerals, *17th*, and watched her own hand make the seven: a little hook at the top, like the neck of a heron, pulled quickly from right to left.

She had never noticed before that she did it.
