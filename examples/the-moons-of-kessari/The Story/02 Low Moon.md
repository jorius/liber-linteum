# 02 Low Moon

The Salt Courts sat at low moon, when Ilu and Thane were both below the world, so that no house could ever claim the moons had leaned its way. After a Closing the two moons set together at dawn, and the sitting began in that grey, moonless hour, with the lamps still lit along the [[The Salt Courts|Terraces]] and the sky above the seven arches the colour of cold ash.

Ama sat on the Keepers' bench behind Mother Hesk with the ledger case on her knees and her left sleeve pulled down over her wrist. She had not slept. Every time she closed her eyes her fingers had gone to the cord on her cuff and counted it again.

The seven houses sat in a shallow horseshoe below the bench, each in its colours: the blue of Varn, the red of Ossary, Tamsk's orange, Quorra's yellow, the green of Lirr, Belcane's deep purple, Hadrel's undyed grey. Heads of house sat on stools. Heirs and stewards stood behind them. Ama had been coming to sittings for three years and she could have drawn the hall with her eyes shut, and this morning it looked to her like a room she had never been in.

"The Wellwright," said the Speaker, and old [[Pell Odrin]] stood up.

He gave the low-moon reading as he gave it every fortnight, in a voice like a rope running over a pulley. The well stood lower than at the last sitting, by a hand's breadth and two fingers. The Rise, reckoned from the level and the draws, was forty-four thousand drams. He sat down. Nobody looked surprised. The well had been falling by a hand's breadth or so every fortnight of Ama's life.

Then [[Teodor Varn]] stepped out from behind his mother's stool, and the hall changed the way a room changes when someone opens a window.

He was perhaps thirty, tall, in a coat of Varn blue so dark it was almost black, and he had the gift of speaking to a whole hall as if he had taken each person in it aside. His mother, [[Orla Varn]], sat in front of him with her hands folded on her stick and her eyes half shut, an old woman carved out of salt.

"No one in this hall wants what I'm going to ask for," Teodor said. "Least of all House Varn. We have friends in Glassreach. We have kin there. But the well has been speaking to us for twelve years, very patiently, and we keep pretending not to hear it." He opened his hands. "We don't cut Glassreach's water because we wish to. We cut it because the Well of Osk has told us to. I ask the Courts to reduce the Glassreach allotment by one part in five, the cut to be sealed on linen at the low moon after the Dusk Closing, so that the city has a full turn of Ilu to prepare."

Ossary's head objected, though not very hard. Quorra asked for more time and was given it: twenty-seven days, until the next Dusk Closing. Lirr agreed with Varn, as Lirr usually did. Hadrel said nothing at all. The proposal was entered, and Mother Hesk wrote it on the sitting roll in her small, upright hand, and Ama watched the pen and thought about which way the hooks were pulled.

---

He came to the Keepers' bench afterwards, while the houses were filing out under the arches into the first heat. Up close he smelled of sandalwood and of water, actual water, as if he had washed his hands in it that morning and thought nothing of it.

"Eldest," he said to Hesk, with a little bow. "My mother has a fancy to see the old Rise figures. The roll of 399 to 401, I think. She says she remembers the Dry Year better than any of us, and she'd like to be sure she remembers it rightly."

"The Loom House lends nothing during an airing," said Hesk.

"Of course." He smiled, and turned the smile on Ama, and let it rest on her for a moment too long. His eyes went down to her left cuff, where the cord showed under the hem of her sleeve. "Pretty knots," he said. "My mother says the Keepers can read a whole year in one of those. I've always thought that must be a heavy thing to carry around on your arm."

Then he was gone, a dark-blue shape walking out into the white morning, and Ama found that she was holding her own wrist.

---

That night she found Hesk at the big loom, not weaving, just sitting with one hand on the beam.

"Did my mother find something," Ama said. "Before she went."

Hesk did not ask what she meant. She did not pretend. Ama would remember that too.

"Your mother," Hesk said, "found out that the world is unjust, and she thought it could be argued with. Go to bed."

Ama went to bed. She lay in the dark and thought about what Teodor Varn had asked for: not any ledger, but *that* one. She thought about the last line on the roll. *Lost on the Salt Road, with the twin of this roll.*

Every ledger the Keepers make, they make twice. One copy stays in the Loom House. The other, the twin, is carried down the Salt Road to the Glass Annex at Glassreach, so that no single fire, flood or thief can ever take the whole of the Courts' memory. Her mother had died carrying the twin of the roll of 399 to 401. That twin was gone.

But the roll before it, 396 to 398, would have gone down the road three years earlier, with some other Keeper, in some ordinary caravan, and arrived. And if someone had sat in the Loom House twelve years ago and patiently pushed Ilen Seyru's hooks the wrong way across two years of Rise rows, perhaps they had not stopped at two years. Perhaps the twins in Glassreach still said what the Loom House copies had said before anyone touched them.

Nine days to Glassreach for a rider. Twelve for a loaded caravan. Twenty-seven days until the Dusk Closing, and the low moon after it, when the cut would be sealed.

Ama got up, dressed in the dark, and went down to the stacks with a shaded lamp. The roll of 396 to 398 was where it should be, on its rack beside the gap where 399 to 401 had been until last night. She copied its Rise rows onto a strip of clean linen, ink and knots side by side, and did not let herself look at the hooks of the sevens until she had finished. They had been pushed.

Then she cut a length of cord from her spool. She did not write Hesk a note. Ink can be scraped off and written over. She knotted one instead and hung it on the loom beam, where Hesk's hand would find it in the morning: *gone to read the twins*, in the plain knots apprentices use for messages, and under it a single long knot, the one Keepers tie when a debt is still open.

Then she went down through the sleeping town to the caravan yards, where the first camels were already complaining, and asked for the quartermaster.
