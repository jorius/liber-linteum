# 01 The Linen-Keeper's Daughter

Paper cracks in the dry. It was the first thing Ama Seyru had ever been taught, before her letters and long before her knots. Paper cracks, parchment curls, and the wind carries off ash. Only linen remembers.

She had heard it so often that she no longer heard it at all, the way she no longer heard [[The Loom House]] breathing around her at night: the tick of the drying lines, the sigh of the old frames, four hundred years of debts shifting on their rollers like sleepers turning over. But she heard it tonight. She heard it in Mother Hesk's voice, which was strange, because [[Mother Hesk]] was asleep three rooms away, and because the thing across Ama's knees was saying the opposite.

It was the night of a Closing. Beyond the courtyard wall [[Ilu]] and [[Thane]] had come up together over the dunes, the great pale moon and the small red one, like an old woman and a child she cannot quite keep hold of. Far below the town the deep water was rising in the [[Well of Osk]], and its breath came up through every crack in the stone: cool, faintly salt, the only damp air Kessari ever knew. On Closing nights the Linen-Keepers hung their ledgers in the courtyard to drink it. Dry linen folded too often splits along its creases. Linen that has breathed a little folds like a sleeve.

Ama was supposed to be hanging them. Instead she sat cross-legged under the lamp with the roll of the years 399 to 401 across her knees, mending its selvedge.

---

Every row a Linen-Keeper writes, she writes twice. Once in ink, along the weave, so that anyone can read it. Once in knots, along the cord sewn into the edge of the cloth, so that a Keeper can read it with her eyes shut.[^1] Ink can be scraped off and written over. A knot can only be cut, and a cut shows.

The cord on this roll had worn through at the hem, and mending it was slow work. You did not retie old knots. You stitched the old cord to a new backing, one knot at a time, and you read each knot with your fingertips as you went so that you would know at once if you had disturbed it. Apprentices learned to read knots blindfolded. Ama had been the fastest in her year. She was not proud of it, exactly, but she was not *not* proud of it.

Her fingers found the rows for the Rise: the water the deep brought up at each Closing, measured by the Wellwrights and owed to no one, the one line in every ledger that was not a debt.

Six. A gap a thumb wide for the empty place. Eight. Two gaps.

*Sixty thousand eight hundred.*

She looked at the ink beside it. ==Forty-one thousand two hundred drams.==

She had misread. She was tired; the lamp was low; she read it again with her eyes closed, and her fingers said sixty thousand eight hundred, as calmly as before. She moved to the next Closing. The ink said forty thousand nine hundred and fifty. The knots said sixty-one thousand one hundred. The next. The next. All through the year 399 and the year 400, Closing after Closing, the ink said the well was failing, a little more each fortnight, a patient sickness gathering in the deep. The knots said the well was well.

Then came the Dry Year, 401, and the knots fell, and for nine Closings ink and cord agreed: the water had truly failed that year. Everyone knew that. The Dry Year was why Glassreach had been on short water ever since, why the Courts spoke of the well the way you speak of an old man coughing in the next room.

But the knots did not show an old man coughing. They showed a healthy one who had caught a bad fever, once.

Ama put her hand flat on the ink, the way you might put a hand on an animal to calm it.

The roll of 399 to 401 had been kept by the Keeper of those years, and the Keeper of those years had been Ilen Seyru, her mother. Ama had learned to read from this hand. She had a strip of linen in her chest with nine lines on it, a letter her mother wrote her once when she was six and Ilen was away on the Salt Road, and she had read those nine lines until the ink had worn pale under her thumb. She knew how her mother made her sevens: a little hook at the top, like the neck of a heron.

These sevens had the hook.

But ink pools where the pen comes down and thins where it lifts, and an apprentice who reads knots in the dark learns to read ink in the light the same way, by its weight. Her mother had drawn her hooks from right to left, a quick pull. These had been pushed from left to right. Slowly. Carefully. Someone had copied Ilen Seyru's hand very well, and they had never once watched her write.

Ama's eyes went, without her quite sending them, to the last row of the roll. She had read that row a hundred times. Every Keeper's death is written into the ledger she kept, because a Keeper's water allotment must be closed like any other debt.

> *Ilen Seyru, Keeper. Lost on the Salt Road at the Cairn of Teeth on the 17th Closing of 401, with the twin of this roll. The road takes whom it takes. No water owed.*

The seven in *17th* had a hook at the top, though that hand had made no effort to look like her mother's. It had been pushed from left to right.

---

"You'll go blind in that light."

Mother Hesk stood in the doorway in her night shawl, small and straight-backed, her white hair down for once. Behind her the courtyard was full of hanging linen, row on row of pale ledgers turning slightly in the damp breath of the well, so that it looked as if the Loom House had filled with patient ghosts.

"Mother. The Rise rows." Ama heard her own voice come out too high, like a child's. "The ink doesn't agree with the cord. Not one row in two years."

Hesk came and looked. She did not touch the roll. Ama would think about that later: how the Eldest of the Loom House, who touched every ledger as if it were a living thing, kept her hands folded in her shawl and only looked.

"Linen frays," Hesk said at last. "Knots slip. You'll find a hundred such in the old rolls."

"Knots don't slip *upward*, Mother."

A slipped knot loses a count. It never gains one. It was the second thing Ama had ever been taught.

Hesk was quiet for long enough that Ama heard the dunes beyond the wall begin, very faintly, to hum. Then she bent, rolled the ledger closed with quick, sure hands, and tucked it under her arm.

"Hang the rest," she said. "The water won't wait for us." At the door she stopped without turning round. "And Ama. Put it away."

Ama sat a long time in the lamplight after she had gone. When at last she looked down, she found that her fingers had been busy without her. The cord at the cuff of her left sleeve, the one every apprentice wears to practise on, was knotted from wrist to elbow: sixty thousand eight hundred, sixty-one thousand one hundred, and on, and on, every true number she had read, tied tight.

She had not decided to disobey. Her hands had decided for her.

[^1]: The knots are counted in clusters, one cluster for each place of the number, with a thumb-wide gap for an empty place. See [[The Linen-Keepers]] and Ama's own working in [[Knot Reckoning]].
