# 04 Glassreach

Glassreach was not built. It was found.

Long before the Well, before the houses, before anyone had thought to count water, something had fallen out of the sky onto the plain the drovers still call the Glass Flats, and burned there for a very long time. When it went out, the sand for half a day's walk in every direction had become glass, heaved up into spires and blades and long translucent ridges like frozen surf. The first people of [[Glassreach]] had not built a city. They had moved into one, and carved doors.

Ama saw it first at sunset on the twelfth day: a forest of light standing on the horizon, every spire lit from inside by the low sun, so that it looked less like a city than like a fire someone had paused. By the time the caravan reached the gates the light had gone blue, and then the lamps came on inside the glass, thousands of them, and the whole city glowed like a lantern that someone had set down in the desert and forgotten.

Up close it was less like a lantern. Up close it smelled of hot stone and too many people. The houses' agents lived high in the spires, where the wind came through. Everyone else lived in the Lees, the low quarter at the roots of the towers, where the light came down through a hundred thicknesses of glass and arrived broken into colours, and the water lines were long.

In the morning Ama walked through the Glass Market on her way to the Annex. She saw a man selling water from a cask with a blue salt seal. He wore no house colours. He had a little brass cup that held a single dram, a day's drinking for a grown woman, and the people in front of his stall were paying for one cupful with coins she had only ever seen in the hands of stewards. *Sweetwater*, the sign said. *From the deep.* A girl of perhaps eight was licking the dew from the shaded side of a glass wall, steadily, the way a cat cleans itself, as if it were simply one of her chores.

Ama stood very still in the middle of the market for a long moment. Then she walked on.

---

It was a Closing that morning, the dawn one, the short and quiet Closing that falls thirteen and a half days after the long one. Ilu and Thane came up together just ahead of the sun, faint and pale in the brightening sky, and out on the Flats the dunes began, very softly, to sing.

Glassreach answered.

She had been told about it and had not believed it. Every spire in the city took up the dunes' note and held it, a deep, clean hum that came up through the soles of her sandals and into her teeth, so that for the length of a long breath the whole city rang like a struck bowl. All through the market people stopped what they were doing and stood with their heads a little bowed, the way you stand when someone is reading out the names of the dead. Then it faded, and the noise of the morning came back, and the girl at the wall went back to her dew.

---

[[The Glass Annex]] was cut into the roots of the oldest spire, down a long stair where the air grew cooler with every step until Ama could see her breath. The keeper was an old woman called Sefa Imre, half blind, with fingers so fine and quick on a cord that Ama, watching her, felt like a beginner again.

"Three ninety-six to three ninety-eight." Sefa ran her hand along a rack and drew out a roll without looking. "Came down the road on the Hadrel caravan, twelfth Closing of 398. I tied its arrival knot myself." She set it on the reading table and unrolled it with a touch like someone smoothing a sleeping child's hair.

Ama laid her own strip of linen beside it, the copy she had made in the Loom House stacks, and began to read.

The twin's ink and the twin's knots agreed. Sixty thousand, sixty-one, fifty-nine thousand eight hundred, sixty-two. A strong, steady well, Closing after Closing, written by a hand Ama did not know, with its sevens made however that Keeper had liked to make them. Beside it, on her strip, the Loom House copy of the same rows sank gently, fortnight by fortnight, from fifty-two thousand to forty-seven. The same rows. The same Closings. One of them had been rewritten, and it was not the one that had spent twelve years in the cold at the bottom of a stair.

"And the next roll," Ama said. Her voice sounded strange in the stone. "Three ninety-nine to four hundred and one."

Sefa did not need to look. "Never came," she said. "The Keeper carrying it was lost at the Cairn of Teeth. We tied a mourning knot for her on the arrivals cord. Ilen, her name was." She turned her clouded eyes towards Ama. "Your knots have the same lean as hers, child. Did you know that?"

---

"I wondered how long it would take you."

Teodor Varn stood at the foot of the stair, with the cold coming off the walls around him like a draught from an open door. He was dusty to the knee. Varn kept wind-skiffs, it was said, that could make the run from the Courts in five days when the wind was right. It must have been right.

"The Annex lends nothing," said Sefa Imre, before anyone had asked her.

"I haven't come to borrow." He walked to the table and looked down at the two strips of numbers side by side, and Ama watched him read them, and watched him not be surprised. "My grandmother used to say that Keepers are the only people on Kessari who believe the past is a debt."

"It is."

"It's a story. A story everyone agrees on, so they can get on with the day." He said it gently, as if he were sorry to be the one to tell her. "Do you know what happens to a city when it learns there's more water than it was told? First it stops paying. Then it stops obeying. Then the Courts send men down the Salt Road with spears, and people die over water that was never scarce at all. I've read the old rolls too. The Long Thirst, three hundred years ago. Two thousand dead in the Lees, and almost none of them of thirst."

"So you sell them thirst instead."

"I sell them order. A number that everybody believes is worth more than a well." He smiled at her, and it was a good smile, warm and a little tired. "Come home and keep House Varn's books, Ama Seyru. You'd be very good at it. You'd have water enough for a garden. You could buy the Lees a fountain out of your wages and feel splendid every time you walked past it."

"No," said Ama.

He nodded, as if she had told him the time. At the foot of the stair he turned.

"Your mother said no as well," he said. "The road takes whom it takes."

Then he climbed up into the light, unhurried, and was gone.

---

For a long time Ama could not make her hands work. When they did, they were shaking so badly that Sefa had to take the pen from her and copy the twin's Rise rows herself, onto a clean strip, in a slow and perfect hand. At the end she tied a short, fat knot into its cord and pressed it into Ama's palm.

"A witness knot," she said. "From the Annex. A twin for a twin."

Ama ran all the way back up through the Lees to the caravan yards. Dela Fenn was standing on a cask in the middle of the yard, counting.

"We turn round at noon," Dela said, without looking down. "Empty casks travel light. If you want to be at the Courts for the Dusk Closing, you'll be walking when the camels sleep."

"I'll walk," said Ama.
