# 07 Epilogue - What Linen Keeps

Half a year later, on the night of a Dusk Closing, Ama Seyru sat in the courtyard of the Loom House with the roll of the years 399 to 401 across her knees, and a pen, and a new length of cord.

She was a Keeper now, the youngest in the house, with her own colour of thread at her cuff: a blue so grey it was nearly the colour of the linen itself. It had been her mother's colour. Nobody had asked her whether she wanted it. The Keepers had simply left a spool of it on her bed the morning after the sitting, and she had sat and looked at it for a long time before she could touch it.

The Loom House had a new Eldest, a patient, heavy-handed woman who had been Hesk's second for thirty years and who ran the house exactly as Hesk had, except that she touched every roll with both hands, and she touched them often. Mother Hesk's name had been struck from the house, at her own asking, and she had gone down the Salt Road with Dela Fenn's caravan in the first week after the sitting. She lived in Glassreach now, in two rooms in the Lees, and spent her mornings in the cold at the bottom of the Annex stair with Sefa Imre, and her afternoons teaching knots to anyone in the Lees who would sit still long enough to learn them.

Her letters came up the road with every caravan. They were never written. They were knotted, in the plain message knots that apprentices use, and they were always short.

*Forty children here can read a cord. Three of them better than you.*

*Sefa says your knots lean like your mother's. Tell her I know.*

*Found the girl from the dew wall. Her name is Tali. She counts everything. Dela wants her for a cask-girl. Over my body.*

Ama kept them all on a peg above her bed, and read them with her fingers in the dark when she couldn't sleep.

---

The rest had happened slowly, the way true things on Kessari mostly happen. The wellwrights had sealed the hole in the glass with new blocks from the Flats, and the water at Hollow Stone had stopped running, and the man in the Glass Market with the little brass cup had gone out of business, or at least out of sight. House Varn's stool stood dark at every sitting. Orla Varn came anyway, every time, on her stick, and sat beside it, and said nothing. Teodor had gone south on a wind-skiff a week after the vote, and nobody had seen him since. Ama found that she thought of him more often than she would have liked to, and not always unkindly, which annoyed her.

Glassreach had its water back. Not more water than anyone needed. Simply as much as there was.

The Keepers had spent five months on the Rise rows. Keepers never scrape and never cut. When a line is false, they strike it through, so that it can still be read, and write the true line beneath it, and tie the reason into the cord. A record of a lie is also a record. So now, all through six years of rolls, Hesk's careful sinking numbers stood with a single neat stroke through each of them, and under each one the knots' number in plain ink, and in the margin the same short note, over and over: *Rewritten in the Dry Year. Restored from the cord.*

There was one line left. Ama had saved it for tonight.

She unrolled the ledger to its last row, where it had always been, where she had read it a hundred times as a child, and drew one stroke through it with her pen, cleanly, so that every word still showed.

> ~~*Ilen Seyru, Keeper. Lost on the Salt Road at the Cairn of Teeth on the 17th Closing of 401, with the twin of this roll. The road takes whom it takes. No water owed.*~~

Then, beneath it, in her own hand, the hooks of her sevens pulled quickly from right to left:

> *Ilen Seyru, Keeper. At the Cairn of Teeth on the 17th Closing of 401 she went out at night to count House Varn's casks, as she had every night of that journey, and she did not come back. What became of her is not known. It was not the road.*

She looked at it for a while. Then she took up the cord and knotted it, every word, into the selvedge, where no ink could get at it, and at the end she tied the long knot that Keepers tie when a debt is still open. Some debts stay open. That is not the same as forgetting them.

---

Beyond the wall the moons were rising together, Ilu huge and pale, Thane small and red and hurrying, and the dunes had begun to sing.

All evening people had been going past the courtyard gate towards the well with lamps in their hands: wellwrights and drovers, stewards in their house colours, children who had been promised all fortnight that they could stay up, old Benedek of Ossary on two sticks with a grandson at each elbow. The stair stood open, as the Courts had said it would. They went down in a long, slow, chattering line, and Ama could hear them below the town, faint and echoing, going quiet one by one as the glass began to hum and the water came up to meet them.

She did not go down. She had seen it. She sat among the airing linen instead, in the cool salt breath of the deep, with the corrected roll across her knees, and let the whole house sway around her in the dark: row on row of pale ledgers turning gently on their lines, every debt the Courts had ever owed, written once in ink for anyone to read, and once in knots for anyone who would take the trouble.

Paper cracks in the dry. Linen remembers.

And whatever linen is made to forget, the glass keeps: a white line in the dark, far down, waiting for someone to go down with a lamp and read it.
