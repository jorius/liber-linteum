# Waiting for Messiah

> [!warning] Written before release
> This piece was written in October 2026. Villeneuve's third *Dune* film, adapting *Dune Messiah*, is announced for December 2026 and has not been released. Nothing here describes the film itself — only the book, and what the first two films have set up.

*Dune Messiah* (1969) is a strange book to follow an epic with. It is short, claustrophobic and mostly made of conversations. Twelve years have passed. Paul is Emperor, the holy war carried out in his name has spread across the known universe at a cost in lives he himself compares to the worst tyrants of history, and a conspiracy of everyone he has defeated — the Bene Gesserit, the Spacing Guild, the Tleilaxu, and his own wife Irulan — is closing around him. Paul sees much of it coming. That is the horror of the book: prescience does not set him free; it shows him every bar of the cage.

## What the films have already changed

The most interesting question is what *Part Two*'s ending does to this story. In the novel, Chani is still at Paul's side in *Messiah*, his partner in everything but name, and her fate is the emotional core of the book. Villeneuve's Chani walked away from the new Emperor at the end of [[Dune Part Two (2024)|Part Two]]. Any film of *Messiah* has to begin from that rupture, and it may make the story sharper: the one person who refused to kneel is also the one Paul cannot stop loving.

The films also kept Alia unborn, so the eerie child of the novels has not yet appeared on screen except in a brief vision of her as an adult. *Messiah*'s Alia is a young woman, and the film can meet her fresh.

> [!info]- Spoilers for the novel *Dune Messiah*
> The conspiracy brings Paul a ghola — a regrown body — of his dead friend Duncan Idaho, trained as a weapon against him. Chani dies giving birth to twins. Paul, blinded, does what Fremen custom asks of the blind: he walks alone into the desert. The book ends with power passing to Alia as regent and the twins, Leto and Ghanima, setting up *Children of Dune*.

What I hope for is simple: a film that is willing to be smaller than its predecessors. *Messiah* is not a war story; it is a story about the morning after one. If Villeneuve trusts that — the quiet rooms, the long conversations, the dread — it could be the most Herbertian of the three.
