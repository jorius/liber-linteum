# Dune: Part Two (2024)

*Dune: Part Two* is the better film of the pair, and not only because things finally happen. Where the first film built a world, this one builds an argument — and then hands the audience a choice about whether to believe it. It is a war epic, a love story and a religious drama, and it is constantly, quietly asking you whose side you are on.

The film picks up almost immediately after [[Dune (2021)|the first]]. Paul and Jessica live among the Fremen of Sietch Tabr. Paul learns to fight, to walk without rhythm, to ride; he falls in love with Chani (Zendaya). Jessica drinks the Water of Life and becomes a Reverend Mother, carrying an unborn daughter who already speaks to her. Meanwhile, on the Harkonnen home world, the Baron grooms his younger nephew Feyd-Rautha (Austin Butler), and on the imperial side the Emperor (Christopher Walken) and his daughter Irulan (Florence Pugh) watch the board with growing unease.

## Chani's eyes

The decisive change from the novel — and the reason the film works — is that ==Chani does not believe==. Herbert's Chani is devoted to Paul; Villeneuve's Chani is a young fighter who has grown up under occupation and recognises a prophecy imported from outside when she hears one. She loves Paul *the person* and distrusts Paul *the story*. That puts a sceptic at the centre of the frame, and it lets the film show us Paul's rise through two pairs of eyes at once: the believers who see a messiah and the woman who sees a boy being turned into a weapon.

Javier Bardem's Stilgar is the other half of that equation. His faith is played, at first, for laughs: every time Paul denies being the Lisan al-Gaib, Stilgar takes the denial as further proof, because a true messiah would be humble. It is funny. Then, late in the film, the same faith fills a cavern with thousands of chanting fighters, and it stops being funny at all. The joke and the threat were always the same thing.

## Giedi Prime in black and white

The film's most startling sequence takes place on Giedi Prime, filmed in infrared black and white under a black sun. Feyd-Rautha's birthday duel in the arena is staged like a fascist rally carved out of chalk and ink; even the fireworks look like spreading stains. Butler, pale and hairless, plays Feyd as a pure appetite, giggling and lethal, and Léa Seydoux's Margot Fenring arrives to assess him as one would a breeding animal. In a few minutes the film tells you more about the Harkonnens than the whole first instalment did.

> [!warning] A critic's caution
> The worm-riding scene is the most purely thrilling image in either film, and that is precisely the danger. Villeneuve makes Paul's ascent ==exhilarating== on purpose. If you leave the cinema cheering for the holy war, the film has caught you in exactly the trap Herbert set for his readers.

## What it cuts

The compression is real. In the novel, years pass among the Fremen; Paul and Chani have a son; Alia is born and grows into an unsettling child. Here, months seem to pass at most, Alia remains unborn, and Paul's transformation after the Water of Life — from reluctant refugee to the man who addresses the war council in the south — is abrupt. Feyd arrives late and has little room to become more than a magnificent threat. Irulan, who in the books narrates history itself through her epigraphs, is mostly a watchful face. These are the costs of telling the back half of a dense novel in one film, and on rewatch they show.

But the cuts serve a design. Without the years of domestic life, Paul's choice becomes a single sharp turn instead of a slow slide, and the film can end on its argument rather than on a coronation. For a fuller book-to-film comparison, see [[Kept, Cut and Changed]].

> [!info]- Spoilers: how it ends
> Paul kills the Baron himself — there is no Alia to do it — and defeats Feyd-Rautha in single combat before the Emperor. He demands Irulan's hand to take the throne. The Great Houses in orbit refuse to recognise him, and Paul sends the Fremen out to make war on them: the holy war he has spent two films dreading begins at his command. Chani, who will not kneel, walks out alone into the desert and calls a worm. The film ends not with a celebration but with her leaving.

That ending is the boldest thing in the film. Herbert's novel closes on a bittersweet note between two women who will never be wives; Villeneuve's closes on a refusal. It turns the next film into a confrontation the novel only implied, and it tells anyone who mistook the first two hours for a triumph exactly how to reread them. I wrote about that reading at length, in Spanish, in [[Cuidado con los mesías]].

## The verdict

Where Part One sometimes stood still, Part Two moves; where Part One was beautiful, Part Two is beautiful *and* troubling. Hans Zimmer's score finds a tender theme for Paul and Chani and then lets it curdle. The battles are vast but always legible. And the central idea — that a story of liberation can become a story of conquest without anyone changing the words — <span data-bg="yellow">lands harder in 2024 than it did in 1965</span>.[^1]

**Verdict:** <span data-color="green">★★★★★</span> — the rare sequel that improves its predecessor by revealing what it was about.

[^1]: The film was originally scheduled for late 2023 and moved to 2024 during the Hollywood strikes of that year.
