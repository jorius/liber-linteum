# Sand, Stone and Thunder

The first thing you notice about Villeneuve's *Dune* is not a character. It is a sound — something between a voice, a horn and the groan of a building settling — and an image of something enormous standing in a landscape that makes it look small anyway. Before the films tell you anything about the Atreides or the spice, they tell you, physically, that this is a universe built at a scale where people barely register. This essay is about how they do it.

## A score that is not quite music

Hans Zimmer's music for the two films is often described as a score, but it behaves more like weather.[^1] There are themes — the wordless chant that hangs over the desert, the bagpipes of House Atreides, a tender line for Paul and Chani in *Part Two* — but much of the time the music is closer to texture: low drones, throat-like vocals, percussion that sounds as if it were struck on stone, and a solo female voice that rises out of the mix like a call to prayer heard across a desert.

What is remarkable is how little of it sounds like a traditional orchestra. Zimmer and his collaborators lean on processed voices and invented or reshaped sounds, so that the ear cannot easily place where the music comes from. That uncertainty is the point. The Fremen are not an orchestra's idea of "exotic"; the music refuses the familiar shorthand for desert peoples and goes looking for something stranger, older and less comfortable.

The Harkonnens get the opposite treatment: <span data-color="red">grinding, mechanical, airless</span>. Their music — when it is music at all — sounds like machinery breathing. On Giedi Prime in *Part Two*, the crowd's chanting and the arena's ritual noise become part of the same oppressive wall.

## Sound as physics

The sound design does at least as much to build the world as the score. A few choices stand out:

- **The Voice.** When a Bene Gesserit uses the Voice, the words arrive doubled and layered, as if several people were speaking from inside one throat. It is a simple idea and it makes an abstract power instantly physical: you *hear* the command bypass the listener's will.
- **The thumper.** The rhythmic pulse that calls a worm is used almost like a metronome for dread. When it stops, the silence is worse.
- **The worm.** The films rarely give the sandworm a roar. Instead, it arrives as a pressure — a deep tremor, sand hissing, the ground itself seeming to inhale. ==You feel the worm before you see it==, which is what the Fremen would experience too.
- **The ornithopters.** Their wings beat with an insect's buzz rather than a helicopter's chop. It is a small decision that makes the technology feel grown rather than built.
- **Silence.** Villeneuve lets scenes drop almost to nothing — wind, footsteps, breath — so that the loud moments land like blows.

> [!tip] Rewatch with your ears
> Try one rewatch where you pay attention only to sound. Better still, close your eyes during the spice harvester sequence in [[Dune (2021)]] and the worm-riding scene in [[Dune Part Two (2024)]]. You will find that most of the scale is in your ears, not on the screen.

## Brutalism in the desert

The production design, led by Patrice Vermette, is built on an idea that critics have often called brutalist: massive, unornamented forms, hard edges, raw surfaces, very few decorative details. The Atreides ships are grey slabs. The palace at Arrakeen is a fortress of stone and shadow, lit by blades of daylight slicing through narrow openings. Even the Emperor's Sardaukar, glimpsed on their prison planet in the first film, perform their ritual among stark, monumental shapes.

This is a useful contrast with Lynch's 1984 film, whose interiors are baroque — dark wood, brass, ornament on every surface, a kind of decadent Victorian industrialism. Lynch's empire feels old and rotting. Villeneuve's feels old and *permanent*, which is more frightening. Ornament suggests that someone, once, cared about pleasure. Villeneuve's architecture suggests that power no longer needs to bother.

The colour palette carries the same logic from world to world:

| World | Palette | What it says |
|---|---|---|
| Caladan | Grey, green, misty | Home, rain, a world that will be lost |
| Arrakis | Ochre, amber, bleached sky | Heat, exposure, endurance |
| Giedi Prime | Monochrome under a black sun | A culture with the colour drained out of it |

The infrared black and white of Giedi Prime in *Part Two*, shot by cinematographer Greig Fraser, is the boldest version of this approach: it makes a whole people look as if they had been carved out of chalk and ink.

## People against mass

Scale in these films is not only about big things; it is about the ratio between big things and people. Villeneuve keeps placing human figures tiny against walls, dunes, ships and worms, and then cuts to faces in extreme close-up. The rhythm — <span data-color="blue">immense, intimate, immense</span> — is the films' visual heartbeat. It also carries their argument. Paul's story is about a person swallowed by something larger than himself: a prophecy, a religion, a war. The images are telling that story long before the dialogue does.

On the largest screens, some sequences open up into a taller frame, so that the image literally grows when the world does. It is easy to dismiss that as a format gimmick. In practice, it does what the old roadshow epics did: it makes the screen itself part of the spectacle, and it turns watching *Dune* into something closer to standing at the foot of a monument than to watching a story about one.

[^1]: Zimmer's score for the first film won the Academy Award for Best Original Score.
