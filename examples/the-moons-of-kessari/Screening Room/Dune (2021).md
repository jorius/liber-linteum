# Dune (2021)

Denis Villeneuve's *Dune* opens with a whisper rather than a fanfare: *"Dreams are messages from the deep."* It is a fitting first line for a film that trusts mood over explanation, and that spends a long time making you feel the weight of a world before it tells you what that world wants. Some viewers find that patience cold. I find it the film's great courage.

The story, for anyone who has not yet crossed the Shield Wall: the Padishah Emperor orders House Atreides to take over the desert planet Arrakis — the only source of the spice melange — from their rivals, the Harkonnens. Duke Leto (Oscar Isaac) knows it is a trap and goes anyway, because refusing an emperor is its own kind of death. His son Paul (Timothée Chalamet), trained by his Bene Gesserit mother Jessica (Rebecca Ferguson) in ways the Duke only half understands, keeps dreaming of a Fremen girl and of a holy war carried out in his name. The trap springs. The film leaves Paul and Jessica in the deep desert, taken in by the Fremen, at roughly the midpoint of Frank Herbert's novel.[^1]

## An overture, honestly labelled

The title card on screen reads *Dune: Part One*, and the film behaves accordingly. It is ==an overture rather than a symphony==: it sets up houses, debts, prophecies and grudges, and then stops just as a conventional second act would catch fire. Its climax — Paul's knife duel with Jamis — is deliberately small, a boy killing a man in the sand and understanding for the first time what that costs. If you came for a full arc, the ending will feel like a door closing in your face. If you came for a world, it feels like a door opening.

What the film does instead of plot is *texture*. The gom jabbar test, with Charlotte Rampling's Reverend Mother hidden behind a veil and Paul's hand inside the black box, is staged as pure physical dread; we feel the pain because Chalamet's face does all the work. The spice harvester sequence — the dust, the thumping tremor, Leto ordering his people off the machine before it is swallowed — is the moment the film announces its scale. And the night of the Harkonnen attack, shields flaring and fire falling across the sky of Arrakeen, plays like a nightmare rather than an action scene.

> [!quote] The Litany against Fear
> "I must not fear. Fear is the mind-killer."
> — Frank Herbert, *Dune* (1965)

Villeneuve keeps the litany and, crucially, lets Paul *fail* at it. He recites it while terrified, not as a mantra of mastery but as a child's prayer. That small choice sets the tone for both films: this Paul is not a superman in waiting. He is a frightened, gifted, very young man being pushed toward something enormous.

## The cast

Chalamet is slight and watchful, almost too delicate for the role on first viewing — and that is the point, as [[Dune Part Two (2024)|Part Two]] makes clear. Ferguson's Jessica is the film's most interesting performance, a woman whose discipline keeps cracking into fear for her son and then hardening again into something colder. Isaac gives Leto a warmth that makes his doom genuinely sad. Jason Momoa's Duncan Idaho is the only character who seems to enjoy being alive, which is exactly why his last stand hurts.

On the other side, Stellan Skarsgård's Baron barely raises his voice. He floats, bathes in black oil, and speaks in low, reasonable tones, which is far more frightening than the cackling grotesque of 1984. Sharon Duncan-Brewster's Liet-Kynes, a role written as a man in the novel, becomes one of the film's moral centres. Zendaya's Chani, though, is mostly a vision in Paul's dreams here: she has more screen presence in the trailers than in the film. That imbalance is one the sequel corrects with real force.

### What lands

- The sense of **scale**: ships like stone slabs, people like dust on them.
- Ferguson's Jessica, the most complicated figure on screen.
- The sound of the desert — and of the Voice.
- A villain who never needs to shout.
:::
:::column
### What lags

- An ending that is, by design, a pause.
- Chani reduced to a dreamlike promise.
- Some exposition delivered in rooms full of people standing very still.
- A cool, controlled tone that can hold the audience at arm's length.
:::

## A film for big rooms

*Dune* reached cinemas in a strange moment. In the United States, Warner Bros. released it in theatres and on its streaming service on the same day, part of a pandemic-era plan for its 2021 slate that Villeneuve criticised in public.[^2] Watching it on a laptop is possible; it is also a little like reading a symphony's score instead of hearing it. This is a film built from mass and silence and low frequencies, and it rewards the largest screen and the loudest sound system you can find. For more on that, see [[Sand, Stone and Thunder]].

It is not a perfect film. It is stately where it could be urgent, and it sometimes confuses solemnity with depth. But it is a rare blockbuster that believes its audience can sit with unease, and it reads Herbert closely enough to plant the seeds of the warning that the second film will harvest. #villeneuve

**Verdict:** <span data-color="orange">★★★★☆</span> — a magnificent first movement, best judged together with the second.

[^1]: Herbert's novel was published as a book in 1965, after being serialised in *Analog*. Villeneuve's two films split it roughly where Paul and Jessica join the Fremen.
[^2]: The day-and-date release applied to the US; in most other markets the film opened in cinemas only.
